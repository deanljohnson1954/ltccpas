Funding Mechanics

Paying for Long-Term Care

Where the money comes from, how long benefits last, and how flexible your payment structure can be — the mechanics every applicant should understand before choosing a policy.

Start Here

Two different questions hide inside the phrase “paying for long-term care.” The first is how the care itself gets paid for — government programs, insurance, or your own capital. The second is how the premium gets paid, and from which asset. This page answers both, in that order, because the second question only becomes interesting once the first one is settled.

What the Care Actually Costs

Planning begins with a number, and most people underestimate it by a wide margin. The figures below are California statewide medians from the most recent CareScout Cost of Care Survey — rates collected from providers between July and November 2025 and published in March 2026. Your own market may run above or below them.

The Number That Matters Is Not Today’s

Care costs have been inflating in the range of three to five percent a year. At three percent, a year of assisted living moves from roughly $84,000 today to about $113,000 in ten years, and a semi-private nursing home year moves from about $146,000 to roughly $196,000. If you are planning for care at 80 or 85, the relevant figure is the one you will actually face — not the one on the page today.

Source of Premium

Your premium does not have to come from new money. In most cases it comes from capital you already hold, simply repositioned — money that is currently sitting in a low-yielding account doing very little, moved into a contract that does two jobs at once.

A Note on IRAs and Qualified Money

An IRA cannot itself own life insurance, and a distribution taken in order to pay a premium is generally taxable — with an additional penalty possible before age 59½. Qualified money can still play a part in a funding strategy, but only after its own tax analysis. Do not treat an IRA as interchangeable with a savings account here.

Section 1035 Exchanges

A properly structured Section 1035 exchange may allow an eligible life insurance or annuity contract to be exchanged for a qualified long-term care contract without current recognition of gain. Eligibility, ownership and insured requirements, surrender charges, outstanding loans, and policy basis all need review before anything is signed.

How the Premium Is Paid

Premiums are funded one of two ways: a single payment at issue, or a structured schedule that spreads the cost over a fixed number of years. The right choice is usually a cash-flow question rather than a coverage question.

Single Premium

Paid once, at policy issue. No further scheduled premiums are due — assuming the policy is issued as illustrated and no later loan, withdrawal, or surrender affects its guarantees. This is the structure most often used for asset repositioning.

Recurring Premium

A structured schedule chosen to fit your cash flow:

  • 5-Pay — five annual premiums
  • 10-Pay — ten annual premiums
  • 20-Pay — twenty annual premiums
  • Annual premiums payable through age 95

Elimination Periods

The elimination period is the waiting period before benefits become payable — a deductible measured in days rather than dollars.

Example Policy Provisions

The periods below describe one carrier’s design. Elimination periods, the way they are measured, and the settings they apply to differ materially from policy to policy. Read the contract you are actually buying.

0 Days · Home Health Care

Benefits for qualifying home health care can begin as soon as day zero. Care and benefits begin together.

90 Days · Facility Care

For assisted living, skilled nursing, and other facility-based care, a 90-day waiting period applies, measured within 270 calendar days.

Two Dates, Not One

Coverage begins when the policy takes effect. Benefits become payable only after claim eligibility is established and any applicable elimination period is satisfied. Those are two different dates, and confusing them is the single most common misunderstanding I correct at the kitchen table.

Care Received Outside the United States

Example policy provisions — other carriers and policies differ. International provisions vary more widely than almost any other benefit feature. Confirm them in the specific contract before relying on them.

  • Facility care received outside the United States, its territories, and Canada may be covered.
  • Non-facility care received outside the United States is generally not covered.
  • Canada is not treated as international coverage under this design: all forms of contractual care are eligible, benefits are reimbursement-based, and exchange rate conversion applies.

Who Pays for the Care: Four Approaches

Once the cost is understood, the question becomes who pays it. Most families default to one of three answers — a government program, a traditional insurance policy, or their own balance sheet — without examining the limitations of each. A fourth approach addresses several of those limitations at once.

Approach 1 of 4

Government Programs

Medicare generally does not cover ongoing custodial long-term care. It may cover limited skilled nursing or home health services when its medical and eligibility requirements are satisfied — typically short-term skilled care following a qualifying hospital stay.

Medi-Cal, California’s Medicaid program, does cover long-term care services, but eligibility turns on income and asset limits. For most families that means spending down assets before any coverage begins.

Government programs are safety nets of last resort. They are not retirement planning tools, and building a plan around them means accepting the loss of assets you spent a working life accumulating.

Approach 2 of 4

Traditional Long-Term Care Insurance

Traditional health-based long-term care insurance was the original solution, and on a pure care-dollars-per-premium-dollar basis it is still often the most efficient product on the market. Its difficulties are real, however, and they have driven a number of insurers out of the business.

  • Premiums can be expensive, particularly for older applicants or those with health conditions.
  • It is widely viewed as “use it or lose it” — if care is never needed, premiums paid generally return no residual value unless a return-of-premium option was purchased.
  • Policyholders have experienced substantial, repeated rate increases, sometimes with limited warning.
  • Underwriting has tightened. Many applicants in their sixties and seventies find they no longer qualify, at precisely the point they begin to want the coverage.
A Correction Worth Making

Traditional policies are frequently described as cancellable. That is not accurate. Tax-qualified individual policies are generally guaranteed renewable: the insurer cannot cancel your policy because your health deteriorated or because you filed a claim, provided the required premiums are paid. What the insurer can do is raise premiums for an entire approved class of policyholders. The risk is a rate increase, not cancellation — and the distinction matters when you compare products.

Approach 3 of 4

Self-Funding

Some clients intend to pay for care out of their own capital. A small number genuinely can. For most, the arithmetic is less comfortable than it first appears.

  • An extended dementia claim can run many years and cost well over $1 million, depending on the care setting and duration. The Alzheimer’s Association reports average survival of four to eight years after diagnosis, with some people living as long as twenty.
  • Self-funding transfers the entire financial risk to the household, with no leverage and no ring-fenced protection for the healthy spouse.
  • Care costs continue to inflate at three to five percent a year, so the exposure grows for as long as the risk goes unfunded.
The Income Problem

A couple with $2 million may feel entirely secure. But if $1.5 million of that portfolio has to be liquidated to pay for care, as much as three quarters of the household’s income-producing capital disappears with it. The assets may well cover the bill. The retirement income built on those assets does not survive the transaction — and the healthy spouse lives with the consequences for years afterward.

Approach 4 of 4

Asset-Based Long-Term Care

Asset-based long-term care — also called hybrid or linked-benefit coverage — is a life insurance policy or annuity contract that permits access to the death benefit or contract value to pay qualifying long-term care expenses. It exists to answer the objection that has always dogged traditional coverage: what happens if I never need the care.

Depending on the policy selected, asset-based coverage may provide:

  • A death benefit to heirs if long-term care benefits are not fully used, subject to the policy’s provisions.
  • Premiums guaranteed on the policy as issued, so there is no exposure to the class-wide rate increases that affect traditional coverage.
  • Funding by repositioning an existing asset rather than committing new money.
  • Extended or lifetime benefit duration on certain policies, or through an extension-of-benefits rider.
  • Benefits generally received income-tax-free under a tax-qualified contract.
Read That List Carefully

Not every asset-based product offers all of those features. Benefit duration, the percentage of the death benefit available for care, cash value behavior, and premium guarantees all vary by carrier and by policy. This category is not one product, and the differences between contracts are larger than the marketing suggests.

Before You Select a Policy

Whichever approach you choose, these are the provisions that determine what you actually own. Take this list to any conversation you have — with me or with anyone else.

  • Claim triggers: inability to perform two of six Activities of Daily Living, or severe cognitive impairment
  • Effect of loans, withdrawals, or surrender on cash value, care benefits, and death benefit
  • Monthly benefit amount and maximum benefit period
  • Carrier financial strength ratings and claims-paying history
  • Inflation protection — whether it is included, and at what rate
  • Underwriting standards — and the importance of applying while insurable
  • Reimbursement versus indemnity (cash) benefits
  • Whether the contract is tax-qualified
  • Whether benefits are shared between two insureds
  • California Partnership asset protection, where applicable
  • Whether premiums are waived while benefits are being paid
  • How benefits coordinate with any coverage already in place

Why a CPA Leads This Conversation

Long-term care is sold as an insurance decision. It is really a capital allocation decision with an insurance product attached, and that is an accountant’s question before it is an agent’s.

  • The self-fund-versus-insure question gets modeled, not asserted. If your balance sheet genuinely carries the risk, I will tell you so.
  • Which asset should fund the premium is a tax question. Repositioning a CD, a nonqualified annuity, and an IRA distribution produce three entirely different results.
  • Section 1035 exchange eligibility gets evaluated against the actual contract — basis, loans, surrender charges, and ownership — rather than assumed.
  • Where a corporation pays the premium, deductibility depends on policy design, the insured’s relationship to the company, and applicable compensation and shareholder rules. Eligible premiums may be deductible; the answer is specific to the entity and belongs with the corporation’s tax adviser, working from the actual contract.
  • I hold no carrier quota and no product allegiance. On a pure care-dollars-per-premium-dollar basis, traditional coverage often wins — and I will say that even though it is not what I most often place.
