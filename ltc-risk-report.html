The Long-Term Care Risk Report

The risk, the arithmetic, and whether you can afford to carry it yourself.

Most Americans carry insurance for events they hope never happen and statistically may never face. They insure automobiles against accidents that, for most drivers, never occur — or that resolve in a matter of days and cost thousands of dollars, not hundreds of thousands. They insure homes against fires that, in most cases, never materialize. They buy travel insurance, umbrella policies, and identity theft protection — coverage for events whose probability in any given year is often well below one percent.

Yet most American families — including many financially sophisticated professionals — carry no protection against the one risk that is, by any actuarial measure, more likely to occur than all of the events they routinely insure against: the need for long-term care.

That is rarely the result of ignorance. It is more often the result of underestimation — a combination of optimism, avoidance, and the assumption that Medicare, personal savings, or family members will absorb the cost when the time comes. None of those three assumptions survives contact with the arithmetic.

The Short Answer Long-Term Care Fails Every Test of a Self-Insurable Risk

A risk can reasonably be carried on your own balance sheet when four things are true: the probability is low, the cost is bounded, the timing is foreseeable, and the worst case can be absorbed without changing the plan. Long-term care satisfies none of the four. This page sets out what the risk costs in California, what self-funding it actually requires, and the specific points at which the self-funding argument breaks down.

The Probability

Most People Underestimate How Likely This Is

The self-funding argument usually rests on an unstated assumption: that care is unlikely, or that if it comes it will not last long. The data does not support either half of that assumption. Roughly 70 percent of Americans who reach age 65 will require some form of long-term care (U.S. Department of Health and Human Services). That is not a tail risk. It is the actuarial expectation for the majority of the population. Approximately one in five will face more than $200,000 in lifetime long-term care costs, and about one in five will need care for five years or longer.

The distribution matters more than the average. Many people need modest help for a short period. A meaningful minority need years of paid, high-intensity care — and it is that minority, not the average, that determines whether a household’s plan survives.

The self-insurance strategy works when the risk is low-probability and the cost is bounded. Long-term care is high-probability and unbounded. That combination is precisely what insurance exists to address.

The Inflation Problem

Your Portfolio Must Outrun an Accelerating Cost

Self-funding depends not only on having sufficient assets today, but on those assets growing fast enough to keep pace with the cost of care. Long-term care costs have risen roughly 3.84 percent a year over the past three decades — faster than general inflation — and the recent movement has been sharper: assisted living roughly 10 percent over a recent twelve-month period, nursing home costs 7 to 9 percent across 2024 and 2025, home health aide costs about 3 percent. If care inflates at 4 percent annually and a portfolio earns 5 percent after tax, the real cushion is one percent a year. A sequence of below-average investment years, or a market drawdown at the wrong moment, eliminates that margin entirely.

Projected forward at a conservative 3 percent, today’s California private-room median of $15,178 a month becomes roughly $20,400 a month in ten years and roughly $27,400 a month in twenty. A plan built on today’s cost of care is not a plan for the year care is likely to be needed.

The Timing Problem

Care Events Do Not Arrive on Schedule

Care needs arise unpredictably — following a stroke, a fall, a cognitive diagnosis, or a gradual decline that accelerates without warning. The financial demands begin immediately and do not pause for markets to recover. Sequence-of-returns risk, already the central hazard of a drawdown portfolio, is amplified when a care event forces large, unplanned withdrawals in a down year.

A care event that arrives during a market correction is not an edge case. It is a foreseeable scenario that a self-funding strategy has to survive — and most portfolios are not sized to handle both at once.

The Hidden Cost

Self-Funding Is Often Transferred to the Family

When a self-funding strategy proves insufficient, the shortfall is rarely absorbed in the abstract. It is absorbed by family members. Adult children take on caregiving roles, reduce their working hours, and draw on their own savings; informal family caregiving remains the single largest source of long-term care support in the United States. And Medi-Cal, California’s Medicaid program, requires spending down personal assets to program eligibility levels before it will pay. For a household with meaningful retirement savings, a home, or a surviving spouse who depends on those assets, Medi-Cal is not an alternative plan. It is what happens after the self-funding plan has already failed.

The Underwriting Reality

You Cannot Buy Coverage After the Risk Materializes

The most consequential feature of the self-funding decision is that it forecloses the alternative in a way that cannot be reversed. The practical window for obtaining coverage is typically a client’s fifties and early sixties. Households that decide at 65 to self-fund, then reconsider at 73 after a health event or a closer look at the cost projections, often find that the coverage they might have bought is no longer available to them at any price. Every category of coverage is medically underwritten, and approval is never assured. The underwriting window, once closed, does not reopen.

Four Points That Belong in Any Retirement Analysis

What Most Plans Get Wrong

One

Medicare Does Not Cover Extended Care

Medicare is built for acute and rehabilitative care. It provides limited skilled-nursing coverage and no meaningful coverage for custodial long-term care — which is the type most families will actually need.

Two

Most Care Happens at Home

The majority of long-term care today is delivered at home, not in a facility. Benefit structures limited to facility-based care may significantly underperform against how care is actually received.

Three

Women Require Care Longer

Women live longer on average and require care longer — 3.7 years against 2.2 for men. Planning that treats a couple as a single averaged life understates the exposure on one side of the household.

Four

The Cost Lands on the Household

An unstructured care event does not affect only the person receiving care. It erodes the portfolio, disrupts the income strategy, and transfers a disproportionate burden to the healthy spouse and adult children.

The Self-Insurance Question

Two Objections, Examined

Among financially sophisticated families — those with meaningful retirement assets, professional incomes, and careful financial habits — the idea of self-funding long-term care risk has real surface appeal. If you have accumulated enough, why pay premiums when you could simply pay for care yourself? It is a reasonable question, and it deserves a rigorous answer, because the assumptions inside it are more fragile than they appear. What follows is not ideological. It is arithmetic.

Objection One “I can self-fund the risk and pay for it myself.”

Long-term care is usually framed as an asset question. It behaves as an income question. Consider a married couple with $2 million of income-producing capital supporting their retirement. If an extended care event consumes a large share of that capital, the income it was producing falls with it — and the healthy spouse is left with a fraction of the income the retirement was built around, at the point in life when they are least able to rebuild it. The household may have had enough on paper. What it did not have was a structure that kept the income intact while the care was paid for.

Illustrative. A $1.5 million care event against $2 million of income-producing capital would reduce that capital, and the income it generates, by roughly three-quarters. Figures of this magnitude assume an extended dementia claim beginning years from now, with medical cost inflation compounding in the interim.

Objection Two “Average care is only two to three years — why insure for life?”

Coverage is not held against the average. It is held against the catastrophe. The risk of a prolonged dementia event should be treated the way the risk of cancer is treated: you would not accept a health plan that excluded it on the grounds that most people do not get it. Buying long-term care coverage sized to the average is like buying fire insurance that covers small kitchen fires — the average is what happens to most people, and the catastrophe is what financially destroys a family.

The Alternative

What Transferring the Risk Actually Costs

The case against self-funding is not complete until it is priced. The relevant comparison is not premium against zero — it is premium against the exposure the premium removes.

A single premium of roughly $203,900 for a healthy couple, both age 50, is about seven months of combined care at today’s California private-room median.

Long-term care benefits with no cap on the number of years, subject to the policy’s monthly benefit limit and eligibility requirements.

Guaranteed death benefit to heirs — $156,100 more than the premium paid, generally income-tax-free under current law.

Put plainly: the single premium is roughly what seven months of combined care would cost out of pocket today. In exchange, the insurer assumes a lifetime obligation — whether the care event lasts two years or twenty-five. Self-funding is the better arithmetic only if you know, with certainty, that neither spouse will ever need more than about seven months of care. No one knows that. And if long-term care is never needed, the capital is not consumed. The policy pays a guaranteed $360,000 death benefit to heirs — $156,100 more than the premium paid, or roughly 1.77 times the outlay, generally income-tax-free under current law. The capital is repositioned rather than spent.

Premium and death-benefit figures are drawn from a single-premium whole life illustration for a healthy couple, both age 50, California. Long-term care benefit payments reduce the policy’s death benefit and cash surrender value. Guarantees are subject to the claims-paying ability of the issuing carrier. This is one structure among several; premium deductibility for a business entity depends on the entity type and the policy’s tax-qualified status and should be reviewed before it is relied on.

My CPA Perspective

When Self-Funding Is the Right Answer — and When It Is Not

For some households, self-funding is genuinely the correct decision. If a decade of care for both spouses would not change the retirement plan, the income it produces, or what passes to the next generation, then the capital is doing its job and there is no case for a premium. I will say so.

For most households I review, the honest question is not whether they can afford a premium. It is whether the retirement plan can absorb a multi-year, high-cost care event arriving at the worst possible moment, lasting longer than any projection assumed, and costing more each year than it cost the year before — while the healthy spouse still needs the income that plan was built to produce. That question is answered by modeling both paths side by side on your own numbers, not by an argument in either direction. My compensation does not vary by carrier, by product, or by whether you buy anything at all.
